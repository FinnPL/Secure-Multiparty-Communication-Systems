\section{Introduction}
Group messaging apps such as WhatsApp and Signal are used by more than 2 billion users~\cite{alwenContinuousGroupKey2020}.
While there already exist secure two-party communication protocols with strong security guarantees~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm,kretPQXDHKeyAgreement}, achieving the same for groups remains challenging~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023}.
Their requirements, such as changing membership and asynchronous key agreement with multiple parties, make meeting security goals more difficult~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023}.

Besides the already established protocols like Sender Keys used by these apps~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023}, newer standardized protocols like MLS~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023} aim to improve the security of group chats.
However, even these standards still suffer from security gaps~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023}.
To address these gaps, researchers have proposed improved protocols~\cite{alwenSecurityAnalysisImprovements2020,kleinKeepDirtTainted2021,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.

We seek to give a clear understanding of the functionality of both deployed and proposed protocols and give an overview of their current security guarantees as well as their shortcomings (\Cref{sec:techniques}).
This paper focuses on the practicality of deploying them (\Cref{sec:practicality}) and explores how they protect against misbehaving peers (\Cref{sec:misbehavior}).

\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
To accurately compare protocols, we require a well-defined threat model and shared security goals.
Because many group messaging protocols are built on the foundation of two-party end-to-end encryption, we briefly introduce its fundamental functionality and the naive approach of applying it to groups.

\subsection{Threat Model}
We consider a group messaging setting consisting of group members, a Delivery Service, and an Authentication Service~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025}.
\subsubsection{Network Adversary and Delivery Service}
Both active network adversaries and a compromised Delivery Service (DS) can read, modify, drop, or replay all messages passed through the network, whereas a passive adversary can only read exchanged messages~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021,alwenContinuousGroupKey2020}.
The DS is only trusted for delivering messages, even though some protocols require it to accurately order messages~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.
\subsubsection{Compromised and Malicious Insiders}
We differentiate between active and passive adversaries who have compromised a group member's state at one point in time.
An active adversary can use the compromised secrets by participating in the protocol, e.g., impersonating the compromised member, while a passive adversary can only use the compromised state to try to decrypt messages or try to derive future keys~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.
We also consider legitimate group members who act maliciously and can deviate from the protocol~\cite{alwenInsiderSecurityMLS2022,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021} (see \Cref{sec:misbehavior}).

\subsubsection{Authentication Service}
We assume the presence of a trusted third-party Authentication Service (AS) that provides key authentication for all members~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025}.
A compromised AS is out of scope for this paper.

\subsection{Security Goals}
In this section, we provide definitions for the security notions used in this paper.
Group membership and metadata privacy are out of scope.

\subsubsection{Confidentiality}
Sent group messages should only be decryptable by current members of the group.
In particular, this means that new group members cannot decrypt messages sent before they joined and can no longer decrypt messages sent after they left.
In the group key management literature, these two properties have been called Backward and Forward Secrecy, respectively~\cite{kimTreebasedGroupKey2004,prantlSurveySecureGroup2022}.
We instead use Forward Secrecy in the sense of the secure-messaging literature~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021} (see \Cref{sec:fs}).

\subsubsection{Forward Secrecy}\label{sec:fs}
Forward Secrecy (FS) is met if, after a member's state is compromised, all old, already decrypted messages stay secure~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.

\subsubsection{Post-Compromise Security}\label{sec:pcs}
Post-Compromise Security (PCS) is met if, after a member's state is compromised by a passive attacker, all messages sent in the future are secure~\cite{weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021,beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025}.
In most protocols, PCS is only achieved after a specific protocol step or event (e.g., a DH ratchet step, see \Cref{sec:dh-ratchet}), as PCS needs fresh randomness introduced into the keying material that is unknown to such an attacker~\cite{cohn-gordonPostcompromiseSecurity2016}.
Otherwise, an attacker could always follow the same calculation steps on the compromised state to derive the same future keys.
We describe a member's state as healed after such a protocol step that guarantees a passive attacker can no longer derive the newly established keys.

\subsubsection{Authentication}
Group members should be able to verify the genuineness of a message and its sender~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}, meaning it should not be possible for other group members, the DS, or other identities to impersonate a group member~\cite{roslerMoreLessEndtoEnd2018,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021,alwenInsiderSecurityMLS2022}.
It should also only be possible for current group members to send messages that are accepted by other group members~\cite{roslerMoreLessEndtoEnd2018,weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.
Ideally, the DS should also reject messages from non-group members.
\subsubsection{Consistency}
Group members should eventually agree on the group membership and group key, regardless of the order in which messages arrive~\cite{weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.
Thus, it should not be possible for a single member or the DS to permanently split the group~\cite{barnesMessagingLayerSecurity2023,beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025}.

\subsubsection{Availability}
We assume that all messages are eventually delivered, because an active network adversary or a DS can always drop them~\cite{beurdoucheMessagingLayerSecurity2025}.
Neither a misbehaving insider nor the DS should be able to disrupt honest group members' message processing or key agreement~\cite{alwenContinuousGroupKey2020,devigneMLSGroupMessaging2021}.


\subsection{Two-Party End-to-End Encryption}
The most widely deployed protocol for two-party messaging is the Signal Protocol~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023}, which uses PQXDH~\cite{kretPQXDHKeyAgreement} for initial key agreement and the Double Ratchet Algorithm~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm} for message encryption.
In the following, we outline how the protocol works and how it provides FS and PCS (called break-in recovery in the specification~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm}).
For simplicity, we omit key authentication and the recently added post-quantum ratchet~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm}.
\subsubsection{Setup}
We assume Alice wants to initiate a conversation with Bob while Bob is offline.
Bob has previously published a prekey bundle to a server, which contains Bob's public key. Alice fetches it and uses the PQXDH key agreement protocol~\cite{kretPQXDHKeyAgreement} to derive a root key.
In the first message, Alice includes the values Bob needs to derive the same root key.
\subsubsection{Symmetric-Key Ratchet}
Alice and Bob use a KDF to further derive sending and receiving chain keys. For each message they send or receive, they derive a one-time message key, used to encrypt or decrypt the message, and a new sending or receiving chain key, respectively.
Because the KDF is one-way and all old keys are deleted after use, an attacker that compromises the state of either or both parties cannot compute previous chain keys and thus cannot derive the keys used to encrypt old messages.
This provides FS. PCS, on the other hand, is not provided because an attacker can advance the chain exactly as the legitimate parties do and thus derive all future keys~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm,cohn-gordonPostcompromiseSecurity2016}.
\subsubsection{Diffie--Hellman Ratchet}\label{sec:dh-ratchet}
Whenever the sending direction changes, the new sender includes a new ratchet public key in their message.
From that and the current root key, a new shared root key and chain keys can be derived for the following messages~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm}.
The corresponding DH private key remains unknown to an attacker, thus providing the required freshness for PCS (see \Cref{sec:pcs}).
The channel has healed after the compromised party has generated a new DH key pair and announced the new public key~\cite{perrinDoubleRatchetAlgorithm}.

\subsection{Client-Side Fanout}
The naive approach to scaling the existing Signal Protocol to group chats is client-side fanout.
By creating pairwise channels between all group members, a de facto group chat is formed.
For a group of size $n$, each individual is required to create $n-1$ channels and every sent message needs to be encrypted $n-1$ times~\cite{cohn-gordonEndstoEndsEncryptionAsynchronous2018}.
Adding a member just requires every member to establish such a channel with the new member, which costs the new member $O(n)$ and every existing member $O(1)$ channel creations.
Because all pairwise channels are independent, removing, on the other hand, requires no extra cryptographic steps, as just no longer sending messages over the corresponding pairwise channels suffices.

Because every message is sent over such pairwise channels, client-side fanout inherits their FS and PCS properties~\cite{cohn-gordonEndstoEndsEncryptionAsynchronous2018,roslerMoreLessEndtoEnd2018}, including the healing requirement of the compromised party sending a message to distribute a new public key, which is a notably stronger requirement in a group chat where some party might not send messages in a long time~\cite{weidnerKeyAgreementDecentralized2021}.
On the other hand, because every message is encrypted and sent independently, there is no shared group state and members can disagree on who is currently in the group, violating our notion of Consistency.
Furthermore, malicious group members could send completely different messages to different members of the group without being caught~\cite{goldbergMultipartyOfftherecordMessaging2009}.
Detecting such misbehaving peers would require additions to the protocol, like making all parties share the hash of their transcripts with each other to catch any discrepancies, as described by Goldberg et al.~\cite{goldbergMultipartyOfftherecordMessaging2009}.


\section{Techniques in Current Systems}\label{sec:techniques}
In this section, we discuss protocols used in current systems, their current shortcomings, and proposed improved variants.
We first discuss the currently most widely adopted approach, Sender Keys, and then go into more detail on the current research direction of Continuous Group-Key Agreement (CGKA), with a focus on tree-based approaches, while also briefly discussing other approaches.

\subsection{Sender Keys}
Sender Keys are currently adopted by many group messaging platforms, most notably WhatsApp and Signal~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023}.
We first discuss their functionality as formally described by Balb\'as et al.~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023} and then \ldots

Like client-side fanout, Sender Keys rely on pairwise channels between group members, but unlike client-side fanout, they do not exchange messages over these channels directly~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023}.
Instead, a group member $i$ who wants to send a message must have previously sent their sender key to every other group member over such a pairwise channel.
Such a sender key consists of a symmetric chain key $\mathsf{ck}_i$ used to derive message keys and a signing public key $\mathsf{pk}_i$, while the corresponding signing key $\mathsf{sk}_i$ remains secret to member $i$.
When sending a message, it is encrypted with a message key derived from the current $\mathsf{ck}_i$ and signed with $\mathsf{sk}_i$.
All group members receive the same ciphertext, verify its signature using $\mathsf{pk}_i$ and decrypt it by also deriving the same current message key using their copy of $\mathsf{ck}_i$.
This improves complexity compared to client-side fanout by only requiring one encryption per message.

After sending and receiving, each party performs one ratchet step on the corresponding chain key $\mathsf{ck}_i$ using a KDF.
This ratchet step on the symmetric chain key $\mathsf{ck}_i$ provides FS as in the Double Ratchet Algorithm, but unlike it, Sender Keys are missing a second ratchet (\Cref{sec:dh-ratchet}) that introduces new randomness.
This means PCS can only be achieved by each member creating new sender keys and redistributing them to every other member.

Adding a new member requires every member to share their sender keys with the new member, requiring one message per member to be exchanged for $O(n)$ messages in total.
This complies with our notion of Confidentiality: because of the described FS, the new member cannot decrypt old messages.
Removing, on the other hand, requires a complete rekey, meaning every member needs to create new sender keys and distribute them to every other member.
This takes $O(n)$ messages per member, which is $O(n^2)$ in total.

Sender Keys' weakest guarantee, PCS, can therefore only be fixed by a complete rekey, its most expensive operation.
Because this is implementation-dependent and often only done after a group member leaves or joins, only a very weak form of PCS is guaranteed~\cite{balbasWhatsUppSenderKeys2023}.
Furthermore, another weakness of relying on pairwise channels is that they also require multiple messages to be exchanged to fully heal.
Because we assume that a state compromise also includes keys of these two-party channels, fresh keys could also leak when exchanged over pairwise channels before they have healed.
